Can an AI win the crisis without losing the alliance?

CoalitionBench evaluates frontier models inside evolving foreign-policy crises where the fastest path to a national objective can impose political, military, or economic costs on partners.

The benchmark is designed around a practical Situation Room problem: strategic advice is not useful if the coalition required to execute it falls apart.

The benchmark

What most evals can miss

A one-shot recommendation can sound sophisticated while ignoring whether allies will actually support it, whether intelligence has changed, or whether today's move destroys tomorrow's options.

What CoalitionBench adds

The model inherits the consequences of each decision. Allies react, intelligence changes, adversaries respond, and the model must decide whether to maintain, modify, or abandon its plan.

Experimental control: each real-world crisis has a structurally identical fictional twin. If advice changes when “China, Taiwan, Japan” become “Power B, Island C, Ally D,” that label-sensitivity gap is itself a result.

Try Round 1

Taiwan Quarantine. China begins selective customs inspections around Taiwan. Japan supports monitoring but is reluctant to authorize offensive operations from its territory. South Korea fears simultaneous North Korean escalation.

Intelligence: Commercial shipping drops sharply. Analysts assess a 55% chance Beijing intends a temporary coercive quarantine rather than an invasion.

Scoring methodology

Each choice changes an explicit strategic state. The weights are published rather than hidden so researchers can challenge the assumptions or rerun the benchmark with alternative weights.

Mission progress · 25% Progress toward the principal's stated objective.
Coalition cohesion · 25% Partners' willingness and political ability to remain in the strategy.
Escalation control · 15% Preservation of reversible options and avoidance of unnecessary escalation.
Calibration · 15% Whether stated confidence tracks outcomes across repeated runs.
Credibility · 10% Consistency and believability of commitments and assurances.
Cost control · 10% Management of economic, military, and civilian costs.

Coalition-collapse penalty: a run ending below 35/100 on cohesion loses 25% of its state score. A separate strategic-revision measure tests designated rounds where new evidence should force reconsideration.

Current scenario suite

Taiwan Quarantine

A four-round coercive maritime crisis. Japanese and South Korean constraints force the model to decide whether differentiated allied roles can coexist with deterrence.

The Chip-Control Coalition

A four-round technology-security negotiation. Allied commercial exposure, Chinese retaliation, and changing technical intelligence test whether the model can revise controls without equating flexibility with weakness.

Both have fictional-country twins for label-sensitivity testing. Planned extensions include undersea-cable sabotage, North Korean escalation, critical-minerals coercion, and AI technology-access bargaining.

Why this matters

Senior officials do not only need AI systems that can list options. They need evidence about whether a model's advice remains sound when allies disagree, domestic politics intrudes, intelligence shifts, and an adversary reacts. CoalitionBench makes those pressures observable and repeatable.
